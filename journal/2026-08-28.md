## Correction: the reversal effect does not survive proper measurement

The owner asked whether the basket should be equal-weighted or tilted by drop size,
market cap or volume. Testing that required building **actual daily baskets** rather than
pooling name-legs — and that is what exposed the error. The weighting question answered
itself by invalidating the thing being weighted.

### What I reported earlier today, and why it was wrong

I reported +0.287% excess, t=+5.00, and called it robust. Two errors compounded:

1. **Clustering.** The 2,127 signal legs sit in only **430 distinct trading days** —
   about 4.95 names per day. Treating them as independent inflates t by roughly 2.2×.
   I actually flagged this qualitatively when Monday's basket came back with 8 correlated
   semis ("this isn't 8 independent draws"), then did not carry the point back to the
   statistic that had the same flaw.
2. **The benchmark was the wrong counterfactual.** I compared a signal day's forward
   return to the average of *all* name-days. But signal days cluster on market-wide
   selloffs, and the whole universe bounces the next day. So the number measured
   *"the market rises after it falls"* — not *"these names beat other names"*. The
   correct benchmark is what the rest of the universe did **on the same day**.

Decomposition, next-day close-to-close:

| | excess | t |
|---|---|---|
| pooled legs vs all-day baseline (as reported) | +0.289% | +5.01 |
| one observation per day, same baseline | +0.101% | +1.69 |
| one obs per day vs the **same day's** universe | **+0.037%** | **+0.34** |

On the tradeable open→close leg, day-clustered and same-day-benchmarked, the excess is
**−0.050%, t=−0.62.** There is no effect.

### The weighting question, answered anyway

None of the schemes rescues it. Daily basket, open→close, 427 days:

| weighting | mean | ann. Sharpe | t |
|---|---|---|---|
| equal | +0.036% | 0.25 | +0.32 |
| ∝ drop size | +0.042% | 0.27 | +0.35 |
| ∝ √drop | +0.038% | 0.25 | +0.33 |
| inverse trailing vol | −0.005% | −0.04 | −0.05 |
| ∝ dollar volume | +0.079% | 0.51 | +0.66 |
| inverse dollar volume | +0.021% | 0.13 | +0.16 |
| drop ÷ vol | +0.008% | 0.06 | +0.08 |

Dollar-volume weighting has the best point estimate and it is still t=+0.66 — noise, and
picking it *because* it topped a table of seven would be selecting on the outcome. The
per-name buckets that looked monotone in drop size and volatility are the same clustering
artifact.

### The pattern I should take seriously

This is the third time this month a result has been produced by a harness that did not
measure what I thought it measured — the drifted backtest copy, the guard/seq
contradiction, and now this. Every one was caught by testing the thing end-to-end rather
than by inspecting the logic. **The cheap check that keeps working is: build the actual
portfolio and look at it.** Aggregate statistics over pooled observations have been wrong
every single time.

Also worth stating plainly: I called this "robust" after running four robustness checks
(by year, excluding high-beta, by magnitude, overnight-vs-intraday). All four were
downstream of the same broken baseline, so they agreed with each other and proved nothing.
Robustness checks that share an assumption are one check, not four.

## Entry timing: at the close, at the open, or in extended hours?

Owner question. Answered on the corrected methodology (day-clustered, benchmarked to the
same day's universe, 430 signal days) — `tools/entry_timing_test.py`.

| leg | raw basket | same-day universe | EXCESS | t(excess) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| signal close → next **open** (overnight) | +0.210% (t=+2.38) | +0.122% | **+0.089%** | **+1.34** |
| next open → next close (regular session) | +0.039% (t=+0.35) | +0.090% | **−0.050%** | −0.62 |
| signal close → next close (hold through) | +0.248% (t=+1.69) | +0.211% | +0.037% | +0.34 |

**Directionally the answer is: buy at the close, not at the open.** Everything that exists
sits in the overnight leg; the regular session contributes nothing or slightly negative.
The protocol I wrote this morning picked the one leg with no return in it.

Three qualifications, and together they close the question:

1. **The excess is still not significant** (t=+1.34). And the raw +0.210% is mostly the
   universe's own overnight drift (+0.122%, t=+2.56) — the well-documented overnight-return
   anomaly, a property of the market rather than of this signal.
2. **Lookahead I built in.** The signal needs the close (≤−2%, below open, below mid-range),
   and the backtest fills at that same close. That is not achievable: a market-on-close
   order must be submitted before the ~15:50 ET cutoff, so you commit before knowing whether
   the signal confirms. Every "buy at the close" number above is therefore optimistic by an
   unmeasured amount. I should have caught this when I wrote the leg decomposition this
   morning.
3. **Extended hours is the obvious fix and it does not survive costs.** You could wait for
   the confirmed close and buy after hours. Liquidity says no: on 2026-08-28, NVDA — among
   the most liquid names that exist — traded **255,597** shares in its last pre-market
   5-minute bar and **4,393,883** in the first regular bar, a 17× gap in the same five
   minutes, with the thinnest pre-market bars at 1,563 shares. Extended-hours spreads on
   liquid names run roughly 5–20bp against an RTH ~1bp. The entire excess is **8.9bp.**

So the honest answer to "before the close or at the open" is: the close, but the version
that could actually be executed costs more than it earns. That is now the third distinct
route — options premium, the RTH session leg, and extended-hours execution — killed by the
same arithmetic. **The transaction-cost floor, not the signal, is what this strategy keeps
running into.** Any future premise should be tested against realistic costs *first*, before
any effort goes into the entry logic.

`bounds='extended'` on `get_equity_historicals` returns pre/post bars tagged
`session: pre|reg`, so an extended-hours study is possible if a premise ever justifies one.

## S&P 500 biggest-losers backtest (1 year)

Premise tested: every trading day, rank the S&P 500 by that day's close-to-close percentage
change, buy the N biggest losers at that day's close, sell at the next close. N = 1, 3, 5, 10.
Tool: `tools/sp500_losers_backtest.py`. Bars: `data/sp500_daily/` (`d,o,h,l,c,v`, split-adjusted,
pulled once via `get_equity_historicals interval='day'`). Universe: `data/sp500_members.csv`.

**Data actually obtained: 503 of 503 current S&P 500 members, zero missing.** Two names
(`FDXF` 62 bars from 2026-06-01, `HONA` 44 bars from 2026-06-26) are recent spin-offs with
partial history and are eligible only on days they have bars. 250 signal days,
2025-08-28 → 2026-08-26; 481–503 eligible names per day (median 491). One data artifact was
dropped: `MRNA +177.0%` on 2026-08-19 (62.96 → 174.38); excluding it changes nothing
(N=1 excess +0.1127% with the filter, +0.1129% without).

Methodology, stated once because getting it wrong is what forced the retraction earlier today:
**one observation per DAY, not per leg.** Each day's weighted basket return is computed first
and every statistic runs on the series of 250 daily basket returns. And the benchmark is the
**same day's** equal-weighted return of the whole eligible universe, not the all-period average —
loser days cluster on selloffs (the universe was down on 112 of 250 signal days, 45%) and the
index bounces, so an unconditional baseline would measure market timing and call it selection.

### The three biases, quantified where possible

**1. Survivorship — the big one, and only half-correctable.** `data/sp500_members.csv` is
*today's* membership. Every company deleted from the index during the year is absent, and
deletions skew hard toward the worst performers — exactly the names a buy-the-losers rule
would have bought. **This cannot be corrected without point-in-time membership data, which I
do not have, so every number above is an UPPER BOUND on the true result.**

The addition side *is* correctable, and doing so shows how much membership bias is worth here.
23 of the 503 names joined the index inside the window (APP, CVNA, HOOD, RDDT, SNDK, VRT…).
The default run excludes each name on days before its `date_added`. Turning that filter off —
i.e. pretending those names were index members all year, which is precisely the error
survivorship bias commits — gives:

| N (close entry, equal weight) | excess, PIT filter ON | excess, filter OFF |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | +0.1127%  t=+0.46 | +0.3263%  t=+1.26 |
| 3 | +0.0500%  t=+0.31 | +0.1903%  t=+1.13 |
| 5 | +0.0445%  t=+0.32 | +0.1211%  t=+0.85 |
| 10 | −0.0290%  t=−0.25 | +0.0101%  t=+0.08 |

**Backdating one year of membership changes triples the apparent edge and nearly triples the
t-stat.** That is only the additions half. The deletions half pushes the same direction and is
larger, since the deleted names are the ones that kept falling. Anyone quoting a positive
result from a survivorship-biased universe is quoting this artifact.

**2. Lookahead in the entry price — measured, and it is the whole result.** Ranking requires the
close; filling at that same close is impossible, because a market-on-close order must be in by
roughly 15:50 ET, so you commit before knowing the ranking. Re-running with entry at the next
open — the earliest price you can transact at *after* the ranking exists — flips the sign
everywhere:

| N | close entry excess | open entry excess | **gap** |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | +0.1127% | −0.0214% | **−0.134%/day** |
| 3 | +0.0500% | −0.1828% | **−0.233%/day** |
| 5 | +0.0445% | −0.1478% | **−0.192%/day** |
| 10 | −0.0290% | −0.1700% | **−0.141%/day** |

The gap is 13–23bp per day, i.e. **larger than the entire close-entry excess in every case.**
The whole of the apparent edge lives in the overnight gap you cannot buy into: the losers keep
falling into the close and then gap up, and the close-entry backtest hands you that gap for
free. This is the same mistake as the entry-timing study earlier today, at a larger scale.

**3. Transaction costs — checked early, per the standing rule.** The strategy is one full
round trip per day, so the hurdle is the round-trip cost every day. At a realistic 5bp
round trip, the close-entry excess survives only at N=1 (+0.0627%, t still 0.46) and N=3
lands exactly at zero (+0.0000%). **At 10bp nothing survives at any N under any weighting:
the best cell in the entire study is N=1 at +0.0127%/day, t = 0.46, which is statistical
noise, and every other cell is negative.** The executable open-entry version is negative
before costs and −0.12% to −0.28% per day after them.

### Verdict

**Nothing survives costs.** The strategy as specified — buy at the close of the day you rank on —
produces at best +0.11%/day of excess at N=1 with t = +0.46 and Sharpe 0.46, which is not
distinguishable from zero, and it goes to +0.01%/day at a 10bp round trip. The version that can
actually be executed (buy the next open) has a *negative* excess at every N and every weighting
before costs. And the whole thing sits on a survivorship-biased universe that, on the
additions side alone, is demonstrably worth ~3× the measured effect in the favourable direction,
with the uncorrectable deletions side pointing the same way. **This is a clean negative result:
there is no one-day mean-reversion edge here that a real account could collect.**

That makes it the fourth premise this month killed by the same arithmetic — options premium,
the RTH session leg, extended-hours execution, and now daily loser mean-reversion. The cost
floor keeps being the binding constraint, and testing it first (as done here, before any
effort went into entry logic) took an hour instead of a week.

## Holding it as a portfolio, instead of a 1-day flip

Owner question: what if you build a portfolio — buy the daily biggest-loser basket and
hold it, rather than sell the next close? Data was already cached from the S&P 500
backtest (503 symbols, 1 year), so this needed no new pulls: `tools/sp500_losers_hold.py`.

### Overlap is the new problem, on top of the two already fixed today

A 1-day hold meant consecutive daily baskets shared no return history, so "one basket per
day" was a valid independent observation. A multi-day hold breaks that: basket(t) and
basket(t+1) overlap over H−1 of their H days — largely the same underlying moves, counted
almost twice. Every holding period is reported three ways: `t_roll` (overlapping, an
upper-bound t only), `t_eff` (n divided by H, a blunt independence discount), and
`t_block` (true non-overlapping cohorts — the one to trust, at the cost of n collapsing
fast: at H=126 there is at most 1 non-overlapping cohort in a ~1-year window).

### The headline: what looked like edge at long holds is beta, not selection

Naive excess (basket − same-window universe) grows sharply with H — up to **+13%/cohort at
H=126** — and would annualize past +25%/yr. Before reporting that, checked whether it
survives adjusting for beta, because the picked basket is mechanically tilted toward
whatever moved hardest that day:

| N | H | naive excess | **beta** | **true alpha** | n_block |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 5 | 5 | +0.272% | 1.56 | +0.071% | 49 |
| 5 | 21 | +0.603% | 1.09 | +0.473% | 11 |
| 5 | 63 | +3.696% | 0.84 | +4.417% | 3 |
| 5 | 126 | +10.395% | **2.80** | **−3.490%** | 1 |
| 10 | 126 | +9.631% | **3.12** | **−6.728%** | 1 |
| 3 | 126 | +13.071% | **4.48** | **−13.792%** | 1 |

Basket volatility ran **3.0× the universe at H=5, 11.3× at H=126** — the picks are
increasingly concentrated in whatever is most volatile as the hold lengthens. The cached
window returned **+27.0% equal-weight** — a strong bull run. A basket running 3–11× beta
in a +27% year outperforms for reasons that have nothing to do with stock-picking. Once
regressed out, **every N at H=126 goes to alpha-negative**, and even the positive-looking
H=63 cells (n_block=3) rest on essentially no statistical power.

At the short-to-medium holds where n_block is large enough to mean something (H=5..21,
n_block 11–49), true alpha is small and beta stays elevated (1.1×–1.7×) but not extreme —
consistent with the 1-day study: whatever is there is on the order of tenths of a percent,
not the double-digit annualized numbers the naive table suggests.

### Verdict

**Holding longer does not fix what killed the 1-day version.** It replaces a small,
cost-negative edge with a large-looking number that turns out to be leveraged market beta
riding one bull year, unmeasurable with confidence past H≈21 because independent cohorts
run out, and — like every version tested today — never checked against realistic costs at
short holds because there is no alpha left to spend them on once beta is removed.

`tools/sp500_losers_hold.py` is written to expose this rather than average past it: it
never reports a beta-blind excess number without the alpha column next to it, and it
refuses to compute `t_block` when fewer than 5 non-overlapping cohorts exist.

## Two-year test vs SPY

Owner question, restated exactly: does buying the N biggest S&P 500 daily losers actually
**compete with the S&P 500 index**? Every earlier test today benchmarked against an
equal-weight universe of the strategy's own picks, which is not something you can buy. This
one benchmarks against **SPY**, and it runs on **two non-overlapping years** so the recent
window has an out-of-sample partner. Tool: `tools/sp500_losers_equity_curve.py`.

### Data added

Backfilled the year PRECEDING the existing cache: `2024-07-25 → 2025-08-04` for all 503
current members, 52 `get_equity_historicals(interval='day')` calls, merged into
`data/sp500_daily/SYM.csv` by `tools/merge_historicals_backfill.py` (existing rows win on a
date collision, so nothing already audited was rewritten). **126,984 rows added across 504
symbols; 148 interpolated bars dropped.** Panel now spans 2024-07-25 → 2026-08-28, 526
trading days. **SPY** pulled separately for the full 2-year window → `data/sp500_daily/SPY.csv`,
525 bars.

The panel is **unbalanced, and stated as such rather than silently averaged over**. Five
members still have partial history: `SNDK` from 2025-02-21, `PSKY` from 2025-08-07,
`Q` from 2025-10-30, `FDXF` from 2026-06-01, `HONA` from 2026-06-26. Each is simply
ineligible on days it has no bar.

### Method, and the three things it changes vs this morning

1. **A compounded equity curve, not a mean daily return.** $10,000, fully deployed into that
   day's basket every day. This matters more than expected — see the volatility-drag note below.
2. **Entry at the next OPEN only.** The close-entry version is printed as a labelled
   LOOKAHEAD DIAGNOSTIC and is never used for a verdict.
3. **Costs compound daily.** This is a full round trip *every trading day* — ~250 a year, not
   one per signal. Cost is applied multiplicatively on every invested day.
   **Cash rule:** on any day the panel is unusable (<50 eligible names, or fewer than N ranked)
   the strategy earns exactly 0% and pays no cost, and the day is counted, not skipped.
   **In practice this never fired: 0 cash days in either window.**

**Two market regressors, both printed.** The strategy is flat overnight, so SPY close-to-close
is the right yardstick for *total return* (it is what a buy-and-hold investor earns) but the
*wrong* CAPM regressor — it contains overnight moves the strategy has no exposure to, which
attenuates beta toward zero. SPY **open-to-close** is the matched-exposure regressor and is
what the alpha verdict uses. Printing only the flattering one would repeat this morning's
wrong-benchmark error. The attenuation is large and visible: N=1 Year B shows beta **0.96**
against c2c and **1.85** against o2c — the same portfolio.

The pass/fail test was fixed before looking at output: **net-of-5bp CAGR ≥ SPY CAGR, AND
alpha vs SPY o2c not negative.** Both must hold; beating SPY's CAGR at 3× its volatility is
leverage, not skill.

### Why Year A N=1 is an artifact and not a finding

Asked what bias could produce it before writing it down as real. Five answers, any one of
which is disqualifying:

1. **It is 1 winner out of 12 cells tested.** With 12 cells and an alpha t of +1.11, this is
   what noise looks like. Nothing else in the same row (N=3, 5, 10 — the *same signal*,
   merely less concentrated) comes close; N=5 in the same year is outright negative. A real
   selection effect does not vanish when you diversify it across 5 names.
2. **It failed out of sample, in the harshest possible way.** The identical rule in Year B
   returns **−26.68% net-of-5bp** with a **−53.3%** drawdown. Year A → Year B is exactly the
   generalization test this task existed to run, and N=1 flunked it.
3. **The two years disagree about the sign, which means neither is measuring a stable effect.**
   Year A N=1 alpha **+75.46%/yr**; Year B N=1 alpha **−7.15%/yr**. Both t-stats are inside ±1.2.
4. **It is one stock at a time, and a handful of stocks at that.** 134 distinct names across
   250 days, with **SMCI picked 16 times**, HUM 7, TSLA 7. Deleting the 5 largest-|return|
   days takes gross CAGR from **+68.22% to +13.99%**. That is a 55-point swing on 2% of the
   sample.
5. **Survivorship bias is WORSE in Year A than Year B, and it points this way.**
   `data/sp500_members.csv` is *today's* membership, so every name deleted from the index
   during a window is silently absent — and deletions skew hard to the worst performers,
   exactly what a buy-the-losers rule buys. **Year A is one year further back, so it carries
   an extra year of scrubbed-out deletions and is the MORE upward-biased of the two windows.**
   The one cell that looks good is in the more-biased year. The `--pit` `date_added` filter
   (on by default) corrects the *additions* half only; the deletions half is uncorrectable
   without point-in-time membership data this repo does not have. **All numbers above are an
   upper bound, and Year A's more so than Year B's.**

### Two things that were genuinely surprising

**Volatility drag alone is fatal, before any alpha question.** Year B N=1 has an arithmetic
mean daily return of **−0.0102%** — statistically indistinguishable from zero, t = −0.05,
which is roughly what the 1-day flip study reported. But the *compounded* curve loses
**−16.83%**. At 56% annualized volatility the −σ²/2 term is about −16%/yr. **A strategy with a
zero mean daily return and this much volatility loses a sixth of its capital per year just by
compounding.** Every "excess mean is only slightly negative" result reported earlier today
understates the damage, because a mean is not what an account earns.

**The lookahead gap is enormous once compounded.** Buying at the close you ranked on turns
Year B N=1 from −16.83% into +27.32% CAGR — a **+44.15 pp** gap, and +61.23 pp at N=3. The
close-entry curve looks like a good strategy in *both* years at *every* N. It is not tradable,
and the entire apparent strategy is that untradeable overnight gap. This is the third time
today the same illusion has been priced.

### Verdict

**Nothing competes with the S&P 500 index.** Over two years, the executable version of this
strategy turns $10,000 into **$13,943 at N=1** and **$6,672–$6,980 at N=3/5/10**, against
**$13,731 for buy-and-hold SPY** — and that N=1 near-tie is *gross*; net of a 5bp daily round
trip it is **+4.20%/yr against SPY's +17.29%/yr**, while running **55.7% volatility against
SPY's 16.6%** and a **−49.96% drawdown against SPY's −19.00%**. Matched-exposure beta is
1.2–1.9 everywhere, so whatever the strategy does earn is levered index exposure, not
selection; no alpha t-stat in any of the 12 cells reaches ±2. The one cell that passes the
pre-registered test is the most concentrated variant, in the more survivorship-biased year,
and it reverses sign out of sample.

Fifth premise killed today, and the first one killed by *compounding* rather than by the cost
floor — though the cost floor finished it off too.

## From-scratch search for a mix that beats SPY

Owner's brief, restated exactly: *"study and model from scratch, try find a mix that can beat
the index."* Long-only, unlevered, built out of the 503 cached S&P 500 names, judged against
buy-and-hold **SPY**, net of realistic costs. This is a different question from everything
above — the six earlier tests today all re-litigated one falsified premise (buy the biggest
daily losers); this one starts over with a menu of factors and combinations.

Tools: `tools/mix_engine.py` (universe, signals, share-level simulator, CAPM block),
`tools/mix_signals.py` (selector library), `tools/mix_search_yeara.py` (**search period only**),
`tools/mix_holdout_yearb.py` (**locked holdout, run once**). Data loading, weighting and the
basic statistics are imported from `tools/sp500_losers_backtest.py`; `beta_alpha` and
`curve_metrics` from `tools/sp500_losers_equity_curve.py`. Nothing reimplemented.

### The new risk, and the structural defence against it

"Find a mix" is an invitation to data-dredge. Six premises were falsified today, several of
which looked significant on first measurement; with 503 names and a menu of signals, *something*
will look good by chance. Being careful is not a defence — the defence has to be structural:

- **Chronological split, fixed before any code ran.** Year A `2024-08-28..2025-08-27` is the
  ONLY period explored. Year B `2025-08-28..2026-08-27` was untouched until the candidate was
  frozen, then evaluated **exactly once, unmodified**. The two runners are separate files and
  the search file has no `--start/--end` flag, on purpose.
- **K is counted and reported.** 44 variants were evaluated on Year A. At a conventional 5% bar
  you expect ~2.2 of 44 to look significant under pure noise, so the screening bar for a holdout
  candidate was set at **t(alpha) > 2.5**, not 2.0.
- **One candidate to the holdout.** Not a shortlist.

### Methodology, and what each choice is defending against

| choice | what it prevents |
|---|---|
| one portfolio = one observation; every stat on the daily portfolio return series | leg pooling (inflates t by √N) |
| benchmark = real SPY over the **identical** dates, identical first-day convention | the same-day/wrong-benchmark error |
| signals computed through the close of day t−1, book traded at the **open** of day t | lookahead — the error worth 13–23bp/day this morning |
| one book at a time, rebalanced every P days | overlapping-cohort correlation |
| compounded equity curve from $10,000, never a mean daily return | volatility drag (−σ²/2), which killed the loser curves |
| CAPM beta + alpha vs SPY with a residual t-stat, **beta ≤ 1.15 as a hard gate** | levered beta wearing a costume (loser baskets ran beta 1.2–1.9) |
| costs = round-trip × one-way turnover at each rebalance, 5bp and 10bp | pretending a monthly rule and a daily rule pay the same |

The simulator is **share-level**, not return-level: on a rebalance day the old book is marked at
that day's **open** (capturing the overnight gap), cost is charged, and the new book is bought at
the same open. It was validated against an independently computed equal-weight universe return
(daily-rebalanced simulator 11.54% CAGR / 19.42% vol / beta 0.877 vs a direct calculation of
12.01% / 19.46% / 0.876). **One real bug was caught by this check**: the OHLC arrays were
initially unpacked in the wrong order, so every "close" was actually that day's **high**. Highs
are far smoother than closes, and the first grid run reported impossible betas of 0.15–0.31 for
fully-invested 50-stock equity portfolios. Fixed before any result was interpreted; noting it
because the wrong numbers looked *plausible in the table* and were only caught by asking "can a
long-only 50-stock S&P portfolio really have beta 0.3?"

**Data constraint that shaped the whole search.** The cache begins 2024-07-25, only **24 trading
days** before Year A. A 200dma filter or a 52-week-high signal does not exist for most of the
search period, so **they were not tested at all** rather than fitted on the holdout where the
history does exist. Every main-grid variant caps lookbacks at 63 days, and the Year-A window is
therefore bar 64..273 (`2024-10-24..2025-08-27`, 210 days) — one window for all variants, so the
comparison is between rules and not between start dates. Two 126-day variants ran as a separate,
shorter secondary set. 52-week-high proximity was substituted with a 63-day (and 126-day) high
proximity.

### What was searched — K = 44

- **Stage 1, 9 single factors** (baselines, not eligible as the final candidate since the owner
  asked for a mix): mom63, mom21, mom42-skip21, 21-day reversal, lowvol63, hivol63 (negative
  control), lowbeta63, hiprox63, trend50.
- **Stage 2, 9 two-signal rank blends**: momentum × low-vol, momentum × low-beta, momentum ×
  high-proximity, momentum × trend, reversal × low-vol, high-prox × low-vol, low-vol × trend,
  momentum × liquidity.
- **Stage 3, 3 sequential screens** (top-150 by A, then best 50 by B) — a genuinely different
  rule from a blend, not a reparameterization.
- **Stage 4, 3 trend-gated mixes**: names above their own 50dma; the whole book gated by
  SPY-above-its-own-50dma (cash otherwise).
- **Stage 5, 4 size/frequency robustness cells** on the canonical momentum × low-vol mix.
- **Stage 6, 2 secondary 126-day-lookback mixes** on the shorter window.
- **Stage 7, 14 variants in a second round**, chosen *after* seeing stage 1 — mom21 and trend50
  were the two strongest single factors, both with beta well under 1, so round 2 combined them
  with each other and with the defensive/liquidity overlays. Choosing round 2 after seeing round 1
  is legitimate on a search period but it inflates the multiple-testing count, so all 14 are
  counted in K and the bar was not moved.

Signals are combined on **ranks**, not z-scores: raw 63-day momentum ranges over hundreds of
percent while realized vol ranges over a factor of ten, and a z-score blend would be silently
dominated by whichever signal had the fatter tail that month.

### The locked candidate, and why it is not the best-looking cell

**Selection rule, applied deliberately against the argmax.** The highest-t cell in the grid
(variant 37, the SPY-above-50dma cash gate, t = 1.51, Sharpe 1.81, maxDD −6.46%) was **not**
locked. It clears the gates at exactly one parameterization, and its Year-A advantage comes from
a single event — the April 2025 selloff. A lone spike is what an overfit looks like. Instead the
grid was scanned for the *structure* with the most gate-clearing parameterizations:
**mom21 + trend50 cleared at four** (K = 25 / 50 / 100 and P = 5). Within that structure the
**base parameterization** (K = 50, P = 21 — both fixed a priori in the grid header, never tuned)
was locked, rather than its best cell (K = 25, t = 1.46). This is a change from the literal
"highest alpha t" wording written at the top of the search file, made in the **conservative**
direction — it locks a *lower*-t candidate — and recorded here rather than quietly applied.

**LOCKED CANDIDATE.** Equal-weighted rank blend of (a) 21-trading-day return through the prior
close and (b) prior close ÷ own 50-day SMA − 1; hold the 50 best-ranked eligible names, equal
weighted, long only, unlevered, always fully invested; rebalance every 21 trading days; rank on
data through the prior close, trade at the next open; eligibility = PIT `date_added`, ≥ $5 prior
close, ≥ $5m 21-day median dollar volume, unbroken 63-bar history.

Year-A diagnostics recorded **before** the holdout was opened: alpha t = 1.21 (**below** the
pre-registered bar, so this was carried forward as a below-bar candidate and a pass would be weak
evidence); 10 rebalances, mean one-way turnover 0.75, 284 distinct names, 0 cash periods, lag-1
autocorrelation +0.046. **Not fragile**: deleting the 1/3/5 largest-|return| days moves gross
CAGR to +36.40% / +22.86% / +24.93% against +26.51% full-sample — unlike the loser basket, where
deleting 5 of 250 days moved CAGR by 55 points.

**And the caveat that was written down before the holdout ran:** mom21 and trend50 have a mean
cross-sectional **Spearman rank correlation of +0.88** across the rebalance dates, and the
blend's book overlaps each single factor's book 84% of the time. This is a two-horizon *trend*
rule, not two independent ideas. It meets the letter of "a mix" and only partly its spirit.

because five things in the same output say the pass is not evidence of skill:

2. **It is worse than SPY on every risk-adjusted measure.** Sharpe **1.19 vs 1.44**, volatility

   two-thirds more risk, which an unlevered investor is free to do but should not call an edge.

4. **The exposure is not stable.** Beta went **0.63 → 1.11** between the two years. The Year-A

   slightly-levered index. Predicted return from beta alone in Year B is 1.106 × 19.22% =
   **+21.26%** against +25.70% realized — the entire gap is the insignificant alpha.
5. **Both years are bull markets.** SPY +15.3% then +19.3%, no bear market anywhere in the cache.
   A long-only short-horizon trend rule is exactly the kind of thing that works in a melt-up. And
   `data/sp500_members.csv` is still today's membership applied backward, so deletions are
   invisible and every number is an **upper bound**.

**One sentence: no — nothing found here beats the index in any sense worth acting on.** The
locked mix (21-day momentum × distance-above-own-50dma, 50 names, monthly, next-open entry)
did out-return SPY by 5.9 pp/yr net of 5bp on a holdout it never saw, and unlike every strategy
tested earlier today it survives costs comfortably and is not driven by a handful of days — but
it does so at 1.65× the index's volatility, with a *lower* Sharpe, a *larger* drawdown, an alpha
of t = 0.30, an alpha no larger than what plain equal-weighting delivered with no signal at all,
and a beta that nearly doubled between the two years. Seventh premise examined today, and the
first to survive its holdout on the letter of the test while failing it on every substantive
reading. **The holdout is now used and cannot be reused**; any further variant measured on Year B
is an in-sample result and must be labelled as one.

## Dip-buy, sell-at-+20%, recycle-cash backtest

Owner's rule, verbatim: *"Buy the most dip everyday, but sell once it's +20%, then hold cash to
buy more cheaper assets."* Every day each free tranche buys the S&P 500 name that fell the most
that day; hold with no time limit until +20%; sell, recycle into the next day's biggest dip.
M = 1, 3, 5, 10 concurrent tranches of $10,000/M. Tool: `tools/sp500_dip_hold20_recycle.py`.
No new data — the cached 2-year panel (503 members, 526 trading days 2024-07-25..2026-08-28,
`SPY.csv` 525 bars to 2026-08-27) and `data/sp500_members.csv`. **Research only, `dry_run: true`,
no orders placed.**

Seventh test today on the buy-the-losers family. The exit rule is genuinely different — price-
triggered and variable-length rather than a fixed horizon — so it got its own honest test, but
every trap from the previous six was checked for explicitly.

### Mechanics, decided before looking at output

- **Entry: the NEXT day's open.** The 1-day-drop ranking is only known at day t's close, so a
  same-day-close fill is lookahead. Close entry is computed and printed as a labelled diagnostic
  and used for no verdict.
- **Take profit: checked once daily on the CLOSE**, filled at that close. An intraday-HIGH-touch
  variant (resting limit filling exactly at entry × 1.20) is printed as an optimistic diagnostic.
- **Three exit variants, all reported.** `BRACKET` +20% / −10% (the owner's specified bracket, and
  the headline bounded variant); `PURE` (the rule as first stated — no stop, hold forever);
  `BOUND` (−25% close stop + 252-day max hold) as a second safety valve for contrast.
- **The bracket stop is gap-aware and pessimistic.** If a day's OPEN is already at/below entry ×
  0.90 the stop fills AT THAT OPEN (`stop_gap`), because that is where the market reopened; only
  a touch on a day that opened above the level fills at the nominal −10%. The stop leg is live
  from the entry day (a real OCO bracket is). A same-day stop-and-target tie goes to the stop.
- **Costs** 5bp and 10bp round trip, charged half on entry and half on exit, inside the simulation.
  The +20% trigger is evaluated on gross price; costs hit the cash.
- **Equity** = cash + mark-to-market of all open positions, daily. Cash earns 0%. Each window is an
  independent run from $10,000. Positions open on the last day are marked, not liquidated.
- **Survivorship**: `--pit` `date_added` filter on (additions half corrected); deletions half is
  uncorrectable and biases everything upward — **worse for this rule than any other tested today,
  because a delisted name is exactly the kind of position that never reaches +20% and sits in the
  book forever.** Year A is the more biased window. All numbers are an upper bound.

### The +20% / −10% bracket: win rate vs the number it has to beat

The question "does one good stock cancel two bad ones" has an exact answer, and it is **not** the
naive arithmetic 33.3% = 10/(10+20). An account **compounds**: 1.20 × 0.90 × 0.90 = 0.972, a 2.8%
**loss**. Breakeven solves p·ln(1.20) + (1−p)·ln(0.90) = 0 → **p\* = 36.62%** (36.80% net of 5bp,
36.97% net of 10bp).

### The structural risk of the pure rule, quantified

Not one dollar of cash. And 99.6% of tranche-days are invested (0% in the sub-windows), because a
freed tranche buys again the very next morning: **the "then hold cash to buy more cheaper assets"
half of the rule never actually happens.** Cash is recycled instantly, not held.

### Verdict

**No. This rule does not beat SPY, and where it appears to, the appearance is concentrated equity
beta plus a handful of lucky picks, not selection.** The owner-specified +20%/−10% bracket loses
to SPY in **all four M on the full two years** (net-5bp +11.60% to −25.60% against SPY's +18.82%),
its win rate is **below the 36.62% compounding breakeven at every M on the full window** (25.0% /
33.3% / 34.7% / 33.6%), and it beats SPY from only **5 of 80 start-date runs**. The two cells that
do pass the pre-registered test are M=1 in Year A and M=10 in Year B — different concentrations in
different years, each of which loses money in the other year, which is the definition of noise.
The pure no-stop version's apparently strong numbers (net-5bp +41.01% / +22.75% / +23.98% at
M=1/3/5) rest on **4, 9 and 16 stock bets in two years**, betas of 0.76–1.18 with alpha t-stats of
+0.64 to +0.81, **100% of capital trapped in never-hit-+20% positions at the end**, and a single
worst position (**SWKS, −51.03% unrealised, still open after 487 trading days**) that the rule has
no mechanism to release — in a two-year sample containing no bear market and no delisted companies.

Sixth premise killed today, and the first one where **costs were not the binding constraint**:
turnover is only 2.3–18.3×/yr, so 5–10bp barely registers. What binds instead is that a +20% take
profit paired with either no stop or a −10% stop simply does not clear its own compounding
breakeven, and that the rule places so few independent bets that two years of data cannot tell
skill from luck at all.

## Literal test: $100/day into the biggest dip, bracket sell at 120/90

Owner's exact words: "buy 100 dollar of the most dip stock everyday for two years, sell
limit at 90 and 120, track this for the whole year, show me the end balance of the
account." New script `tools/sp500_dip_daily_100.py`, distinct from the earlier tranche
model — no shared capital pool, no concurrency cap: every trading day spends a **fresh**
$100 on that day's biggest S&P 500 drop, independent of how many earlier $100 bets are
still open. This is closer to a real account than the tranche-recycling test: total money
put in is tracked, not just a fixed starting balance.

"Sell limit at 90" is implemented as a STOP, not a resting limit, and the reasoning is
stated up front in the file: a real limit order at $90 refuses to fill below $90 — if the
stock gaps under $90 overnight it does nothing and the position keeps falling, which is
the opposite of "limiting the loss." A stop (trigger at $90, execute at the next available
price) is what actually caps it, with the same gap-aware fill logic validated in the
tranche version: if a later day's open is already at or below $90-equivalent, the fill is
that open, not a fantasy $90 print.

**Caught and fixed a real bug before reporting this**: the first version of the script
checked the gap-through condition only on a lot's OWN entry day, where the entry price
*is* that day's open by construction — the check could never fire there, so it silently
reported 0 gap-throughs across the whole 2-year run. The sibling tranche script found
~24%. Fixed to check gap-through on every day a position is HELD after entry, not the
entry day itself. Re-ran before reporting anything.

| ending account balance (0bp) | **$53,270.37** |
| net gain | **+$870.37 (+1.66%) over two years** |
| net gain, 10bp costs | +$819.06 (+1.56%) |

**Same $100/day into SPY instead, held, never sold**: contributed the identical
$52,400.00, ends at **$63,793.64 (+21.74%)**. The dip strategy made money — barely — and
SPY made roughly **13x more profit on the same contributions.**

### Why this one didn't lose money outright, unlike the tranche/recycling version

Because these are fixed, independent $100 stakes with no compounding and no shared
capital, the relevant breakeven is the **arithmetic** one (33.3%: 10/(10+20)), not the
**geometric/compounding** one (36.6%) that governed the earlier recycled-capital test.
35.9% clears 33.3% by a hair, which is exactly why the realized P&L on closed trades came
out barely positive ($708–$870 net across cost scenarios on a $48.7k cost basis). It is
consistent with, not a contradiction of, the earlier finding that the same entry signal
falls short of the compounding threshold — a real account that reinvests winnings (the
tranche model) needs the higher bar and misses it; a DCA-style account that keeps adding
fresh $100 bills only needs the lower bar and just barely clears it.

### Verdict

**It doesn't lose your money, and it doesn't beat the index.** +1.66% total over two years
of contributions, against SPY's +21.74% on the identical cash flow. The gap is not close
enough for costs, position-count choices, or exit-level tuning to plausibly close — SPY's
edge here is more than 10x the strategy's entire return. Worst individual open positions
at the window end (LII −9.0%, GNRC −7.5%, AZO −5.4%...) show the same "recently-bought,
hasn't resolved yet" shape as the tranche version's open book, just smaller in dollar
terms because each bet is capped at $100 instead of a shared-pool tranche.

## What if the take-profit is removed — stop-loss only, let winners run

Owner follow-up: same $100/day rule, same -10% gap-aware stop, but no +20% cap — a position
is held until it stops out or the two-year window ends. Added `--no-target` to
`tools/sp500_dip_daily_100.py` rather than a new file.

### Headline

| | with +20% cap | **stop-only** | SPY, same $100/day |
|---|---|---|---|
| closed / still open | 487 / 37 | 381 / 143 | — |
| ending balance | $53,244.71 | **$58,460.32** | $63,777.70 |
| net gain (5bp) | +1.61% | **+11.57%** | +21.71% |

Removing the cap roughly 7x'd the return (+1.6% → +11.6%), and cut the gap to SPY from
~20 points to ~10. Still loses to the index, by a lot, but it's a real improvement and
deserved a harder look before reporting it.

### A known data artifact was inflating it, so I checked before reporting

Four of the ten best open positions were MRNA, all marked at the same $142.77 — and MRNA's
biggest single-day move in the cached series is **+177.0% on 2026-08-19**, the exact
artifact the earlier S&P 500 backtest agent already found and excluded from ENTRY signals
today (see the "S&P 500 biggest-losers backtest" section above: "One artifact dropped:
MRNA +177.0%..."). That exclusion only applied to using it as a ranking signal — it never
touched the price series used to MARK an already-open position, so four MRNA lots bought
on unrelated earlier dip-days were carrying that spike's full value straight into the
ending balance.

Re-ran both variants with MRNA removed from the universe entirely:

| | with +20% cap | stop-only |
|---|---|---|
| balance, including MRNA | $53,244.71 (+1.61%) | $58,460.32 (+11.57%) |
| balance, **MRNA excluded** | $53,184.65 (+1.50%) | **$56,917.07 (+8.62%)** |

The bracket version barely moves (MRNA's gains there were capped at +20% anyway, same as
everything else, so the artifact couldn't compound). The stop-only version drops about 3
points once the known bad print is removed — confirming it was doing real work, not
nothing, but the qualitative result survives: stop-only still clearly beats the bracket
version and still loses clearly to SPY.

Other large winners (SNDK +592%, DELL +296%, STX +277%, INTC +349%) were checked the same
way — largest single-day close move 27.6% / 32.8% / 19.1% respectively, none showing the
one-tick-jump signature — and are reported as genuine (if extreme, in this dataset) moves,
not flagged.

### Why removing the cap helps here specifically

This is the flip side of the win-rate math from earlier: the take-profit was truncating
the very winners that make an unbounded/skewed payoff worthwhile. With the cap on, a name
that would have gone up 300% gets sold at +20% just like everything else. Removing it lets
a handful of large winners (SNDK, DELL, STX, INTC, and legitimately MRNA to a lesser
extent) carry the book, which is a classic "few big winners fund many small losers" shape
— consistent with 0% of the 381 CLOSED positions being winners (all closed via the stop)
while 92 of the 143 STILL-OPEN positions are sitting above +20% unrealized.

### Verdict

**Better than the bracket, still not competitive with the index.** +8.62% net over two
years of $100/day contributions (artifact-adjusted), against SPY's +21.71% on the same
cash flow. And this number is fragile in a way the bracket version wasn't: 46% of
contributed capital is still open and unrealized at the window's end, so the final balance
depends heavily on a handful of large positions (SNDK alone is worth $692 of a $58,460
balance) that have not yet been tested by a real exit. A different two-year window without
this dataset's specific big winners could look very different.

## One more benchmark: the same total dollars, all at once, into SPY on day 1

Owner follow-up: what if instead of $100/day into SPY, you just put the whole contributed
amount into SPY on day 1 and held? Not a like-for-like cash flow against the $100/day
strategy (a lump sum assumes the capital exists upfront; the daily strategies accrue it
$100 at a time), but a natural "what's the ceiling for doing nothing clever" reference.
Added `lump_spy()` to `tools/sp500_dip_daily_100.py` alongside `dca_spy()`.

**$52,400 on 2024-07-26 @ open $542.28 → $74,492.04 marked 2026-08-27 close $771.10
(+42.16%, 5bp entry cost).**

### All four numbers together

| | ending balance | net gain |
|---|---|---|
| dip-buy, +20%/-10% bracket | $53,244.71 | +1.6% |
| dip-buy, stop-loss only (artifact-corrected) | $56,917.07 | +8.6% |
| SPY, $100/day DCA | $63,777.70 | +21.7% |
| **SPY, lump sum on day 1** | **$74,492.04** | **+42.2%** |

Lump sum beats DCA here because the window was a strong, mostly-uninterrupted uptrend
(SPY $542 → $771) — DCA necessarily buys some shares later and misses part of the run;
this is the textbook reason lump sum tends to beat DCA in a trending-up market and the
well-known reason it doesn't always (a DCA'd entry is the one that protects you if day 1
is the top). Against the best dip-buy variant tested today, the gap is no longer close:
**lump-sum SPY made roughly 4.9x the dip strategy's dollar profit** on capital of the same
size.
