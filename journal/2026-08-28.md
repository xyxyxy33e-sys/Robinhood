## Correction: the reversal effect does not survive proper measurement

The owner asked whether the basket should be equal-weighted or tilted by drop size,
market cap or volume. Testing that required building **actual daily baskets** rather than
pooling name-legs — and that is what exposed the error. The weighting question answered
itself by invalidating the thing being weighted.

### What I reported earlier today, and why it was wrong

I reported +0.287% excess, t=+5.00, and called it robust. Two errors compounded:

1. **Clustering.** The 2,127 signal legs sit in only **430 distinct trading days** —
   about 4.95 names per day. Treating them as independent inflates t by roughly 2.2×.
   I actually flagged this qualitatively when Monday's basket came back with 8 correlated
   semis ("this isn't 8 independent draws"), then did not carry the point back to the
   statistic that had the same flaw.
2. **The benchmark was the wrong counterfactual.** I compared a signal day's forward
   return to the average of *all* name-days. But signal days cluster on market-wide
   selloffs, and the whole universe bounces the next day. So the number measured
   *"the market rises after it falls"* — not *"these names beat other names"*. The
   correct benchmark is what the rest of the universe did **on the same day**.

Decomposition, next-day close-to-close:

| | excess | t |
|---|---|---|
| pooled legs vs all-day baseline (as reported) | +0.289% | +5.01 |
| one observation per day, same baseline | +0.101% | +1.69 |
| one obs per day vs the **same day's** universe | **+0.037%** | **+0.34** |

On the tradeable open→close leg, day-clustered and same-day-benchmarked, the excess is
**−0.050%, t=−0.62.** There is no effect.

### The weighting question, answered anyway

None of the schemes rescues it. Daily basket, open→close, 427 days:

| weighting | mean | ann. Sharpe | t |
|---|---|---|---|
| equal | +0.036% | 0.25 | +0.32 |
| ∝ drop size | +0.042% | 0.27 | +0.35 |
| ∝ √drop | +0.038% | 0.25 | +0.33 |
| inverse trailing vol | −0.005% | −0.04 | −0.05 |
| ∝ dollar volume | +0.079% | 0.51 | +0.66 |
| inverse dollar volume | +0.021% | 0.13 | +0.16 |
| drop ÷ vol | +0.008% | 0.06 | +0.08 |

Dollar-volume weighting has the best point estimate and it is still t=+0.66 — noise, and
picking it *because* it topped a table of seven would be selecting on the outcome. The
per-name buckets that looked monotone in drop size and volatility are the same clustering
artifact.

### The pattern I should take seriously

This is the third time this month a result has been produced by a harness that did not
measure what I thought it measured — the drifted backtest copy, the guard/seq
contradiction, and now this. Every one was caught by testing the thing end-to-end rather
than by inspecting the logic. **The cheap check that keeps working is: build the actual
portfolio and look at it.** Aggregate statistics over pooled observations have been wrong
every single time.

Also worth stating plainly: I called this "robust" after running four robustness checks
(by year, excluding high-beta, by magnitude, overnight-vs-intraday). All four were
downstream of the same broken baseline, so they agreed with each other and proved nothing.
Robustness checks that share an assumption are one check, not four.

## Entry timing: at the close, at the open, or in extended hours?

Owner question. Answered on the corrected methodology (day-clustered, benchmarked to the
same day's universe, 430 signal days) — `tools/entry_timing_test.py`.

| leg | raw basket | same-day universe | EXCESS | t(excess) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| signal close → next **open** (overnight) | +0.210% (t=+2.38) | +0.122% | **+0.089%** | **+1.34** |
| next open → next close (regular session) | +0.039% (t=+0.35) | +0.090% | **−0.050%** | −0.62 |
| signal close → next close (hold through) | +0.248% (t=+1.69) | +0.211% | +0.037% | +0.34 |

**Directionally the answer is: buy at the close, not at the open.** Everything that exists
sits in the overnight leg; the regular session contributes nothing or slightly negative.
The protocol I wrote this morning picked the one leg with no return in it.

Three qualifications, and together they close the question:

1. **The excess is still not significant** (t=+1.34). And the raw +0.210% is mostly the
   universe's own overnight drift (+0.122%, t=+2.56) — the well-documented overnight-return
   anomaly, a property of the market rather than of this signal.
2. **Lookahead I built in.** The signal needs the close (≤−2%, below open, below mid-range),
   and the backtest fills at that same close. That is not achievable: a market-on-close
   order must be submitted before the ~15:50 ET cutoff, so you commit before knowing whether
   the signal confirms. Every "buy at the close" number above is therefore optimistic by an
   unmeasured amount. I should have caught this when I wrote the leg decomposition this
   morning.
3. **Extended hours is the obvious fix and it does not survive costs.** You could wait for
   the confirmed close and buy after hours. Liquidity says no: on 2026-08-28, NVDA — among
   the most liquid names that exist — traded **255,597** shares in its last pre-market
   5-minute bar and **4,393,883** in the first regular bar, a 17× gap in the same five
   minutes, with the thinnest pre-market bars at 1,563 shares. Extended-hours spreads on
   liquid names run roughly 5–20bp against an RTH ~1bp. The entire excess is **8.9bp.**

So the honest answer to "before the close or at the open" is: the close, but the version
that could actually be executed costs more than it earns. That is now the third distinct
route — options premium, the RTH session leg, and extended-hours execution — killed by the
same arithmetic. **The transaction-cost floor, not the signal, is what this strategy keeps
running into.** Any future premise should be tested against realistic costs *first*, before
any effort goes into the entry logic.

`bounds='extended'` on `get_equity_historicals` returns pre/post bars tagged
`session: pre|reg`, so an extended-hours study is possible if a premise ever justifies one.

## S&P 500 biggest-losers backtest (1 year)

Premise tested: every trading day, rank the S&P 500 by that day's close-to-close percentage
change, buy the N biggest losers at that day's close, sell at the next close. N = 1, 3, 5, 10.
Tool: `tools/sp500_losers_backtest.py`. Bars: `data/sp500_daily/` (`d,o,h,l,c,v`, split-adjusted,
pulled once via `get_equity_historicals interval='day'`). Universe: `data/sp500_members.csv`.

**Data actually obtained: 503 of 503 current S&P 500 members, zero missing.** Two names
(`FDXF` 62 bars from 2026-06-01, `HONA` 44 bars from 2026-06-26) are recent spin-offs with
partial history and are eligible only on days they have bars. 250 signal days,
2025-08-28 → 2026-08-26; 481–503 eligible names per day (median 491). One data artifact was
dropped: `MRNA +177.0%` on 2026-08-19 (62.96 → 174.38); excluding it changes nothing
(N=1 excess +0.1127% with the filter, +0.1129% without).

Methodology, stated once because getting it wrong is what forced the retraction earlier today:
**one observation per DAY, not per leg.** Each day's weighted basket return is computed first
and every statistic runs on the series of 250 daily basket returns. And the benchmark is the
**same day's** equal-weighted return of the whole eligible universe, not the all-period average —
loser days cluster on selloffs (the universe was down on 112 of 250 signal days, 45%) and the
index bounces, so an unconditional baseline would measure market timing and call it selection.

on the strength of topping this table, and the top cell (N=1 close, t=+0.46) would not be
worth acting on even if it had been the only test run.

### The three biases, quantified where possible

**1. Survivorship — the big one, and only half-correctable.** `data/sp500_members.csv` is
*today's* membership. Every company deleted from the index during the year is absent, and
deletions skew hard toward the worst performers — exactly the names a buy-the-losers rule
would have bought. **This cannot be corrected without point-in-time membership data, which I
do not have, so every number above is an UPPER BOUND on the true result.**

The addition side *is* correctable, and doing so shows how much membership bias is worth here.
23 of the 503 names joined the index inside the window (APP, CVNA, HOOD, RDDT, SNDK, VRT…).
The default run excludes each name on days before its `date_added`. Turning that filter off —
i.e. pretending those names were index members all year, which is precisely the error
survivorship bias commits — gives:

| N (close entry, equal weight) | excess, PIT filter ON | excess, filter OFF |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | +0.1127%  t=+0.46 | +0.3263%  t=+1.26 |
| 3 | +0.0500%  t=+0.31 | +0.1903%  t=+1.13 |
| 5 | +0.0445%  t=+0.32 | +0.1211%  t=+0.85 |
| 10 | −0.0290%  t=−0.25 | +0.0101%  t=+0.08 |

**Backdating one year of membership changes triples the apparent edge and nearly triples the
t-stat.** That is only the additions half. The deletions half pushes the same direction and is
larger, since the deleted names are the ones that kept falling. Anyone quoting a positive
result from a survivorship-biased universe is quoting this artifact.

**2. Lookahead in the entry price — measured, and it is the whole result.** Ranking requires the
close; filling at that same close is impossible, because a market-on-close order must be in by
roughly 15:50 ET, so you commit before knowing the ranking. Re-running with entry at the next
open — the earliest price you can transact at *after* the ranking exists — flips the sign
everywhere:

| N | close entry excess | open entry excess | **gap** |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | +0.1127% | −0.0214% | **−0.134%/day** |
| 3 | +0.0500% | −0.1828% | **−0.233%/day** |
| 5 | +0.0445% | −0.1478% | **−0.192%/day** |
| 10 | −0.0290% | −0.1700% | **−0.141%/day** |

The gap is 13–23bp per day, i.e. **larger than the entire close-entry excess in every case.**
The whole of the apparent edge lives in the overnight gap you cannot buy into: the losers keep
falling into the close and then gap up, and the close-entry backtest hands you that gap for
free. This is the same mistake as the entry-timing study earlier today, at a larger scale.

**3. Transaction costs — checked early, per the standing rule.** The strategy is one full
round trip per day, so the hurdle is the round-trip cost every day. At a realistic 5bp
round trip, the close-entry excess survives only at N=1 (+0.0627%, t still 0.46) and N=3
lands exactly at zero (+0.0000%). **At 10bp nothing survives at any N under any weighting:
the best cell in the entire study is N=1 at +0.0127%/day, t = 0.46, which is statistical
noise, and every other cell is negative.** The executable open-entry version is negative
before costs and −0.12% to −0.28% per day after them.

The mean is *below* the median everywhere — the payoff is left-skewed, which is the shape you
would expect from buying falling knives: slightly more than half the days win small, and the
losers occasionally keep crashing (FISV −44.0% on 2025-10-29, then another −7.66% the next day).
Deleting the three worst days lifts every mean, which is the opposite of the "one lucky day

Concentration is another hazard at small N: the N=1 pick was APP on 11 of 250 days, SNDK on 8,
MRNA on 7.

### Verdict

**Nothing survives costs.** The strategy as specified — buy at the close of the day you rank on —
produces at best +0.11%/day of excess at N=1 with t = +0.46 and Sharpe 0.46, which is not
distinguishable from zero, and it goes to +0.01%/day at a 10bp round trip. The version that can
actually be executed (buy the next open) has a *negative* excess at every N and every weighting
before costs. And the whole thing sits on a survivorship-biased universe that, on the
additions side alone, is demonstrably worth ~3× the measured effect in the favourable direction,
with the uncorrectable deletions side pointing the same way. **This is a clean negative result:
there is no one-day mean-reversion edge here that a real account could collect.**

That makes it the fourth premise this month killed by the same arithmetic — options premium,
the RTH session leg, extended-hours execution, and now daily loser mean-reversion. The cost
floor keeps being the binding constraint, and testing it first (as done here, before any
effort went into entry logic) took an hour instead of a week.

## Holding it as a portfolio, instead of a 1-day flip

Owner question: what if you build a portfolio — buy the daily biggest-loser basket and
hold it, rather than sell the next close? Data was already cached from the S&P 500
backtest (503 symbols, 1 year), so this needed no new pulls: `tools/sp500_losers_hold.py`.

### Overlap is the new problem, on top of the two already fixed today

A 1-day hold meant consecutive daily baskets shared no return history, so "one basket per
day" was a valid independent observation. A multi-day hold breaks that: basket(t) and
basket(t+1) overlap over H−1 of their H days — largely the same underlying moves, counted
almost twice. Every holding period is reported three ways: `t_roll` (overlapping, an
upper-bound t only), `t_eff` (n divided by H, a blunt independence discount), and
`t_block` (true non-overlapping cohorts — the one to trust, at the cost of n collapsing
fast: at H=126 there is at most 1 non-overlapping cohort in a ~1-year window).

### The headline: what looked like edge at long holds is beta, not selection

Naive excess (basket − same-window universe) grows sharply with H — up to **+13%/cohort at
H=126** — and would annualize past +25%/yr. Before reporting that, checked whether it
survives adjusting for beta, because the picked basket is mechanically tilted toward
whatever moved hardest that day:

| N | H | naive excess | **beta** | **true alpha** | n_block |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 5 | 5 | +0.272% | 1.56 | +0.071% | 49 |
| 5 | 21 | +0.603% | 1.09 | +0.473% | 11 |
| 5 | 63 | +3.696% | 0.84 | +4.417% | 3 |
| 5 | 126 | +10.395% | **2.80** | **−3.490%** | 1 |
| 10 | 126 | +9.631% | **3.12** | **−6.728%** | 1 |
| 3 | 126 | +13.071% | **4.48** | **−13.792%** | 1 |

Basket volatility ran **3.0× the universe at H=5, 11.3× at H=126** — the picks are
increasingly concentrated in whatever is most volatile as the hold lengthens. The cached
window returned **+27.0% equal-weight** — a strong bull run. A basket running 3–11× beta
in a +27% year outperforms for reasons that have nothing to do with stock-picking. Once
regressed out, **every N at H=126 goes to alpha-negative**, and even the positive-looking
H=63 cells (n_block=3) rest on essentially no statistical power.

At the short-to-medium holds where n_block is large enough to mean something (H=5..21,
n_block 11–49), true alpha is small and beta stays elevated (1.1×–1.7×) but not extreme —
consistent with the 1-day study: whatever is there is on the order of tenths of a percent,
not the double-digit annualized numbers the naive table suggests.

### Verdict

**Holding longer does not fix what killed the 1-day version.** It replaces a small,
cost-negative edge with a large-looking number that turns out to be leveraged market beta
riding one bull year, unmeasurable with confidence past H≈21 because independent cohorts
run out, and — like every version tested today — never checked against realistic costs at
short holds because there is no alpha left to spend them on once beta is removed.

`tools/sp500_losers_hold.py` is written to expose this rather than average past it: it
never reports a beta-blind excess number without the alpha column next to it, and it
refuses to compute `t_block` when fewer than 5 non-overlapping cohorts exist.
